# mathstories
just a story
The rain streaks the tall windows of a wood-paneled faculty lounge, where four mathematicians sit around a heavy oak table littered with coffee cups and printed agendas.
At the head of the table sits Arthur, a senior administrator in a perfectly tailored tweed jacket. He does not have a notepad out, nor does he look at the printouts. Instead, he leans forward, gesturing broadly with his hands, his voice carrying the rehearsed gravity of someone delivering a keynote speech.
"Listen to me, colleagues," Arthur says, his tone urgent but smooth. "This recent surge in automated proof-checkers and LLM-assisted repositories isn't just a technical shift. It is a fundamental threat to the integrity of our field. If we allow these black-box tools to dictate logic, we lose the human soul of mathematics. We must stand united. Our department needs to sign the declaration immediately—absolute restriction of AI tools in all submitted research."
Across from him, Elena, a mid-career researcher with ink stains on her fingers, looks up from a page of dense handwritten scribbles. She frowns slightly, adjusting her glasses.
"Arthur, I understand the philosophical concern," Elena says, her voice measured and quiet. "But the new Lean library updates are just formal verifiers. They don't generate the ideas; they just check the line-by-line topology of the proofs. When I ran the topology proofs from last month's draft through the verifier, it pointed out a massive gap in Lemma 3. It didn't rewrite the paper; it just showed us where the logic failed. Why would we ban a tool that ensures our published data is actually correct?"
Arthur smiles quickly, a practiced deflection, and shifts in his chair. He doesn't look at Elena's notes.
"An elegant point, Elena, truly," Arthur replies, waving a hand dismissively. "But you're looking at the micro-level. As Dean, I have to look at the macro-level—institutional prestige, funding structures, and our legacy baseline. If our peer-review process becomes automated, the entire traditional hierarchy collapses. We cannot have unvetted algorithms bypassing the established committee review. We need a buffer to ensure institutional control."
Beside Elena, Marcus, a young postdoc who has spent the entire meeting quietly typing on a laptop, turns his screen toward the center of the table.
"Actually, Arthur," Marcus says, pointing to a graph on his screen showing network data flow. "If we look at the pre-print servers this morning, three independent labs just published a fully verified solution to the boundary problem we’ve been stuck on for two years. They did it completely open-source, using automated verification pipelines. If we sign a ban today, we aren't protecting our legacy—we're just locking ourselves out of the loop while the rest of the field moves forward without us."
Arthur stares at the laptop screen for a moment, his smile tightening. He doesn't look at the data points or the code on the screen; instead, he pulls his cuffs down and looks at his watch.
"Well, Marcus, data can be interpreted in many ways," Arthur says smoothly, standing up and closing his leather folder. "But a formal committee decision requires a broader strategic consensus, not just immediate trends. I have a budget meeting with the provost in five minutes, so we will have to table the technical debate. Let's get the draft of the declaration finalized by Friday. We need to maintain our position."
He smiles warmly, nods to the room, and steps out into the hallway, leaving the open laptop and the dense pages of equations exactly where they are on the table


The rain has stopped, leaving the faculty lounge dark except for the sharp green and white glow of Marcus’s dual monitors. It is well past midnight. Elena sits cross-legged in an armchair, a cold cup of coffee balanced on the armrest, while Marcus types rhythmically, his eyes reflecting lines of code.
"There," Marcus says, hitting the return key with a sharp click. "The script finished parsing the department's publication archive from the last five years. I ran everything through the Lean 4 formal verifier, just like you suggested."
Elena sets her coffee down and walks over to lean over his shoulder. "Did it clear the 2024 topology papers?"
"The ones you and the postdocs actually wrote? Yeah. Zero errors. The verifier cleared the logic chains in less than three seconds," Marcus says, scrolling down the terminal. "But look at the anomaly report. I flagged any paper where Arthur is listed as the primary corresponding author."
He clicks a file titled fundamental_harmonics_2023.txt. The screen immediately floods with bright red text highlighting a multi-line logic block.
text
[ERROR] Line 412: Topological Step Invariant Failure.
[WARNING] Logical jump detected between Lemma 4.2 and Theorem 1.
[DETECTION] Substitution of non-trivial variable 'alpha' with unverified constant.
Result: PROOF IS UNGROUNDED.
Use code with caution.
Elena stares at the screen, her eyes tracing the equations. She pulls a worn notepad from her pocket, flipping back through years of reference formulas, comparing them to the code on the screen. The room is completely silent except for the faint hum of the computer fan.
"This is the foundation paper for the entire regional grant," Elena whispers, her voice dropping. "The one that funded the new lab extension. He claimed he solved the boundary constraint himself over the summer break."
"He didn't solve it," Marcus says, zooming in on the specific lines of math. "Look at what he did. It’s an intellectual shell game. Right here, between Lemma 4.2 and the main theorem, there is a massive conceptual gap. He couldn't make the mathematical jump work, so he just substituted a placeholder constant and covered it up with forty pages of dense, hyper-convoluted jargon."
Elena rubs her temples, looking at the red error code. "A human peer-review committee wouldn't catch this easily. It takes weeks of manual calculation to untangle that much administrative filler. They just saw his title, saw the length of the manuscript, and signed off on it."
"But a machine doesn't care about a title," Marcus points out, tapping the screen. "The verifier strips away the adjectives and looks strictly at the inputs and outputs. The moment it tries to bridge his logic, the whole thing collapses like a house of cards. This isn't a typo, Elena. This is a deliberate stall. If anyone uses a digital checker on this department, his entire legacy vanishes overnight."
Elena steps back, looking toward the dark hallway where Arthur’s office sits. "That’s why he’s pushing the declaration so hard. He’s not trying to save the 'soul of mathematics.' He’s trying to outlaw the only tool that can prove he doesn't know the math."
Marcus closes the laptop halfway, the glow fading from their faces. "So, what do we do? If we publish our verified preprint tomorrow, we are effectively putting this report on the public record."
Elena looks at her notepad, then back at the laptop. "We don't say a word to him. We just upload our project to the open server at dawn. Let the code speak for itself."
